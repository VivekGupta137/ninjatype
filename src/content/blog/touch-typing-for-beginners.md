Touch typing means finding the keys through learned finger movements instead of searching for each one with your eyes. For a beginner, the goal is a reliable movement pattern. Start slowly, learn a small set of keys, and use them in ordinary words before adding more.

NinjaType’s [free touch typing lessons](/learn/) offer separate practice for index, middle, ring, and pinky fingers. Each lesson covers both hands and shows the relevant key map.

## Start from a comfortable home position

On a standard QWERTY keyboard, the left hand starts on A, S, D, and F; the right starts on J, K, L, and semicolon. F and J usually have raised marks that help you locate the position without looking. The thumbs can reach the space bar.

Rest lightly rather than pressing the home keys down. Reach for a letter and return to the same starting position. Keep the keyboard close enough that you do not have to stretch forward to use it comfortably.

The [home-row finger chart](/blog/home-row-typing-finger-placement/) gives the letter assignments used in NinjaType. If you use a different keyboard layout, follow a map for that layout rather than applying QWERTY positions blindly.

## Learn a few keys at a time

Begin with the [index-finger lesson](/learn/fingers/pointer/). Use the map to learn the reaches around F and J. Move slowly enough to notice which finger is pressing each key.

Then try [middle](/learn/fingers/middle/), [ring](/learn/fingers/ring/), and [pinky](/learn/fingers/pinky/) practice. You do not need to finish every lesson in one sitting. Revisit a finger when its keys interrupt ordinary words.

If a key feels unfamiliar, checking the map briefly is fine. Return your eyes to the text for the next attempt. Covering the keyboard immediately is optional; the important change is reducing repeated searching as the movements become familiar.

## Practise consistency before speed

Use the same finger for the same letter during a drill. Press gently and avoid moving the entire hand across the keyboard for one reach. A brief accurate attempt is more useful than a fast run where every word needs correction.

[Aalto University’s study of thirty typists](https://www.aalto.fi/en/news/ten-fingers-not-needed-for-fast-typing) found consistent finger assignments and limited hand movement among characteristics associated with faster typing. It also found fast self-taught typists who did not use all ten fingers. The study does not prove that one technique guarantees a particular WPM.

If your existing technique is comfortable and accurate, you can improve an unreliable movement without replacing everything at once. Expect a temporary slowdown when learning a new assignment; compare progress after several sessions rather than abandoning it after the first attempt.

## Move from finger drills to fresh words

Finger exercises simplify the task so you can focus on movement. Ordinary passages add reading, spacing, and transitions between fingers. Both kinds of practice matter.

After a short lesson, open [solo typing practice](/) and choose a familiar dictionary. Type at a controlled pace and look for the movement you just learned. If the same reach fails, return to the lesson briefly and test it again in a fresh passage.

NinjaType’s finger lessons focus on their displayed letter and punctuation keys. They are not a complete numbers-and-shortcuts curriculum. Practise additional symbols in real text or a local document when those are part of your daily work.

## Know when to add a race

Try racing once you can complete a short passage without constantly searching for keys. [Ghost Race](/ghost-race/) lets you compare your pace with a projection of your saved best attempt. [Multiplayer](/multiplayer-typing-race/) adds friends and a shared countdown.

Keep the finger assignment even when another player pulls ahead. If competition makes you abandon it, spend more time in calm passage practice before returning to races.
