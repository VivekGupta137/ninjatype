**Touch typing means finding keys through learned movements without looking at the keyboard for each letter.** Your eyes can stay on the source text or screen while your fingers use familiar key positions. It is a skill you develop through practice, not a requirement to type at a particular speed.

If you currently search for keys, begin with [NinjaType’s free finger lessons](/learn/). They show the keys assigned to each finger pair on a QWERTY keyboard.

## How does touch typing work?

Repeated practice helps you associate a letter with a consistent movement. Instead of locating J visually each time, you learn where it sits relative to a familiar hand position.

The useful change is reducing repeated searching. You still need to read the passage, notice errors and make deliberate decisions when a key is unfamiliar. Touch typing is not simply typing as quickly as possible with your eyes closed.

This article explains the term and how to choose a starting point. For a full lesson sequence and practice routine, use the [touch typing beginner guide](/blog/touch-typing-for-beginners/).

## What is the home row in touch typing?

On a standard QWERTY layout, the conventional starting positions are:

| Hand | Little finger | Ring finger | Middle finger | Index finger |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Left | A | S | D | F |
| Right | Semicolon | L | K | J |

The raised marks on F and J help you find the position by feel. Your thumbs can reach the space bar. Rest lightly, reach for another key and return to the familiar position.

These assignments are for QWERTY. Another layout needs its own map. The [home-row placement guide](/blog/home-row-typing-finger-placement/) explains the reaches used in NinjaType’s lessons.

## Do you have to use all ten fingers?

Conventional touch typing teaches assignments across both hands, with thumbs commonly used for space. But using ten fingers is not the definition of being fast or accurate.

[Aalto University’s study of thirty typists](https://www.aalto.fi/en/news/ten-fingers-not-needed-for-fast-typing) found fast self-taught typists as well as people with formal training. Consistent finger assignments and limited hand movement were associated with faster typing in that study. The findings do not prove that one technique guarantees a particular WPM.

For a beginner, the conventional layout gives a clear learning structure. If you already type comfortably, improving an inconsistent reach may be more useful than replacing every habit at once.

## What is the difference between touch typing and hunt-and-peck?

Hunt-and-peck describes visually searching for keys, often with a small number of fingers. Touch typing relies more on familiar positions and repeated movements.

The distinction is about how you find the keys, not a strict WPM boundary. A self-taught typist may mix methods, and a new touch typist can be slow while learning. Measure progress through fewer searches and errors as well as speed.

## How do you start learning touch typing?

Start slowly with the [index-finger lesson](/learn/fingers/pointer/), locating F and J. Add the [middle](/learn/fingers/middle/), [ring](/learn/fingers/ring/) and [pinky](/learn/fingers/pinky/) lessons as those reaches become familiar.

Checking the on-screen guide when needed is fine. Then return your eyes to the passage and repeat the movement. After a short drill, use [ordinary word practice](/) to check whether it transfers to real text.

There is no fixed time to learn or guaranteed speed increase. Keep sessions comfortable and compare similar tests over time. If your main concern is slow progress or repeated errors, follow the [typing improvement checklist](/blog/how-to-get-better-at-typing/).
