Typing games can make adult practice easier to return to when each game has a specific job. Use a personal-best race for pacing, a multiplayer round for performing under pressure, and a finger drill for a movement that needs attention. Keep an ordinary typing test as the way to check whether the practice transfers.

NinjaType offers these methods in the same browser. Start with [Ghost Race](/ghost-race/) if you want a solo challenge without waiting for another player.

## Match the game to the skill

| Training method | Use it for | Open it |

| Solo typing test | Baseline speed, accuracy, fresh vocabulary | [Practice](/) |
| Finger exercise | Consistent key reaches | [Learn](/learn/) |
| Personal-best pace race | A controlled opening and steady pace | [Ghost Race](/ghost-race/) |

A game gives you feedback and a reason to try again. It does not automatically correct technique. Use the result to choose a focused exercise between challenges.

## Try a personal-best pace challenge

Complete a valid saved typing attempt, then open Ghost Race. Your opponent is a projection of the average pace of your best valid result, applied to a fresh passage. It is not a live person or a recording of individual keystrokes.

Choose 15, 30, or 50 words. Begin at a pace you can control, and try to keep reading ahead without rushing corrections. Finish the track even if the ghost gets ahead; the attempt can still show what interrupted your rhythm.

The goal is to practise against a pace target. To measure improvement fairly, also run [comparable typing tests](/blog/typing-speed-test-wpm/) with the same duration and dictionary.

## Give a multiplayer round one purpose

Create a room with friends and agree on a skill for the session. A sixty-second race can focus on steadiness; a short Words race can focus on accurate passage completion.

Everyone uses the room’s shared mode, length, and dictionary. Compare WPM and accuracy after the round, then choose one change for the rematch. The [friends training guide](/blog/typing-games-with-friends/) suggests group exercises without changing the app’s official winner rule.

If other players make you rush, return to solo practice for a controlled attempt before racing again.

## Keep vocabulary relevant to your work

Random-word practice helps with letter movement, but daily writing also involves punctuation, unfamiliar words, and editing. In solo practice, broaden the dictionary or choose quotes when you want more varied text.

For specialized work, type a short appropriate passage in a local document too. This might be an email paragraph, technical terms, or a small piece of code. NinjaType’s Hacker Typer generates a visual code stream; it is not an accuracy-scored programming exercise.

Do not compare a specialized passage directly with an easy-word sprint and conclude that your skill declined. They ask for different movements and reading effort.

## Use a short mixed session

Try a warm-up, one finger or accuracy drill, one game, and a brief result review. The exact duration is your choice. Keep the session short enough that you can notice and correct the habit you are practising.

For example, if the race reveals repeated ring-finger mistakes, open the [ring-finger lesson](/learn/fingers/ring/) before trying another track. If the problem is a hurried opening, keep the same track length and deliberately start more slowly.

## Judge the routine by repeatable results

Review [history](/history/) and your own notes after several sessions. Look for useful changes in typical speed, accuracy, and comfort. A personal best is enjoyable, but it is not the only evidence that practice helped.

If progress has stalled, use the [typing plateau guide](/blog/typing-speed-plateau/) to change one variable. If you are learning the keyboard itself, use the [beginner routine](/blog/typing-practice-for-beginners/) before making races the main activity.
