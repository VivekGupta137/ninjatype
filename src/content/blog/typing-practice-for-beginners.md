Beginner typing practice works best when each session has a small purpose. Learn a few keys, type them accurately, then use them in a fresh passage. A speed test tells you where you are; a focused drill gives you something specific to improve.

Here is a suggested ten-to-fifteen-minute routine using [NinjaType’s lessons](/learn/) and [typing practice](/). Adapt the timing to your attention and comfort. It is a schedule to try, not a guarantee of a particular score after a set number of days.

## Choose one skill for the session

If you are learning the keyboard, begin with [home-row placement](/blog/home-row-typing-finger-placement/). If you know the locations but miss a particular key, choose that finger’s lesson. If your typing is accurate but uneven, choose a short passage and practise a steady pace.

Keep the task narrow. “Use the same finger for this reach” is something you can check. “Become a fast typist today” is not a useful instruction for your hands.

## Follow the practice loop

| Part of the session | Suggested time | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Easy warm-up | 2 minutes | Type familiar words without chasing a score |
| One finger or key group | 3–4 minutes | Follow the lesson map and make accurate reaches |
| Fresh passage | 3–5 minutes | Apply the movement among ordinary words |
| Short check and review | 2–3 minutes | Finish a test and note one recurring mistake |

Choose an easy dictionary for the passage at first. The purpose is to combine movements, not to test your vocabulary. Later, broaden the word list or try quotes so you practise reading unfamiliar text too.

Pause between attempts. If you are no longer reading carefully, finish the session rather than adding more distracted repetitions.

## Progress through the finger groups

NinjaType has four lesson groups, each covering both hands. Start with [index fingers](/learn/fingers/pointer/), then explore [middle](/learn/fingers/middle/), [ring](/learn/fingers/ring/), and [pinky](/learn/fingers/pinky/) practice.

You can spread these across several sessions. Move on when you can locate the displayed keys reliably at a comfortable pace, and return to a group when passages reveal a weak reach. The app’s best scores and completion feedback help you revisit previous work; they do not replace your own observation of technique.

The [touch typing guide](/blog/touch-typing-for-beginners/) explains how to keep your eyes on the text while learning these movements.

## Use an accuracy checkpoint

Choose a personal accuracy target before starting a comparison test. For example, you might try to finish at 95% or better while learning a new group. This is a suggested practice checkpoint, not an official proficiency level.

If speed rises while accuracy drops, slow down for the next attempt. If both are stable, try a slightly faster pace or a less familiar passage. Change one thing at a time.

For repeated errors, use the [accuracy-practice method](/blog/improve-typing-accuracy/) rather than repeatedly restarting until you happen to get a clean score.

## Add a game when the keys feel familiar

Once you can complete a short passage without constantly searching, try [Ghost Race](/ghost-race/). It projects the average pace of your saved best attempt onto a new track, so you have a target without needing another player.

For a group session, use [multiplayer typing games with friends](/blog/typing-games-with-friends/). Keep the vocabulary and duration comfortable. A friendly race can motivate another attempt, but the finger lesson remains the place to work on an unreliable movement.

## Keep a small record of progress

After a completed practice test, check [history](/history/) and write a note such as “right index reaches felt easier” or “rushed longer words.” Compare similar tests across sessions rather than treating different dictionaries as interchangeable.

When you want a broader plan, the [how-to-type-faster guide](/blog/how-to-type-faster/) connects testing, drills, accuracy, and racing into one training cycle.
