A typing speed plateau means your results seem to stop improving. Before adding more practice time, check whether the tests are comparable and identify what limits your correct typing: repeated errors, searching for keys, uneven pacing, or unfamiliar vocabulary.

Use [solo practice](/) to measure the issue and [Ghost Race](/ghost-race/) to test a change against a projection of your saved best pace. A plateau is a reason to investigate your routine, not proof that you have reached a fixed personal limit.

## Check whether the plateau is real

Compare several completed tests with the same duration and dictionary. A single personal best can make ordinary results look disappointing even when your typical pace is becoming steadier.

Record accuracy as well as WPM. If raw speed is unchanged but corrections are less frequent, something useful may already be improving. If the dictionary or test length changed, do not treat the score difference as pure loss of speed.

The [typing-speed measurement guide](/blog/typing-speed-test-wpm/) explains a simple baseline check. Use [history](/history/) to review similar sessions rather than combining every mode into one conclusion.

## Identify your current bottleneck

| What the attempts show | A useful experiment |
| --- | --- |
| Frequent mistakes and corrections | Lower the pace and practise [accuracy](/blog/improve-typing-accuracy/) |
| Searching for a particular key | Revisit its [finger lesson](/learn/) |
| Fast start, then long pauses | Practise a more controlled opening |
| Common words feel easy, other words do not | Change vocabulary in solo practice, keeping duration fixed |
| Scores drop late in a long session | Shorten the session and compare when rested |

You do not need the app to diagnose a cause automatically. Add a short observation to the numerical result: “lost my position after this reach” is more actionable than “bad test.”

## Change one part of the routine

Choose one experiment for several sessions. For example, spend a few minutes on a troublesome finger before a fresh passage, or deliberately slow the opening of a timed test.

Keep the comparison test unchanged. If you increase practice duration, switch dictionaries, and replace your finger assignments together, you will not know which change affected the result.

You can use brief faster bursts as an experiment, followed by an accurate steady passage. Stop accelerating when repeated corrections take over. The goal is to bring controlled movement back into normal text, not to produce one flattering short score.

## Use Ghost Race as a pacing target

Ghost Race creates a rival from the average pace of your best valid saved typing attempt. That pace is projected onto a fresh 15-, 30-, or 50-word track. It is not a replay of that attempt’s exact words, pauses, or errors.

Complete a valid saved attempt first if you have no pace available. Then choose a comfortable track length and race your own target. Watch whether you can keep reading and correcting calmly when the ghost appears ahead.

Because the saved pace may come from different text or settings, beating it is a game target rather than a perfectly controlled scientific comparison. Use like-for-like solo tests alongside it when measuring progress.

## Evaluate the experiment, not every fluctuation

After several sessions, compare your typical results and accuracy. Did the original mistake become less frequent? Can you hold the new pace through fresh words? Did the change help only on a memorized drill?

If it helped, keep it and choose the next issue. If it did not, revise the diagnosis. More repetitions of an ineffective routine are not automatically more useful practice.
