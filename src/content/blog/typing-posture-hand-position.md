**Comfortable typing posture is a setup you can check in under a minute: sit so your forearms are roughly level with the keyboard, keep your wrists straight rather than bent up or down, and let your fingers rest lightly on the home row.** Posture does not raise a WPM score by itself, but discomfort is a common reason a practice session ends early or turns tense and error-prone.

This guide gives you a quick setup routine you can run before [typing practice](/) or a [finger lesson](/learn/). It describes general workstation guidance, not medical advice. If typing already causes pain, numbness, or tingling, stop and talk to a healthcare professional rather than training through it.

## Why posture matters for typing practice

Touch typing depends on repeatable movements: the same finger travels from the same home position to the same key. That is easier when your hands start from a stable, relaxed position each time. A keyboard that is too high, a chair that is too low, or wrists bent against the desk edge changes the angle of every reach.

You will usually notice the effect as fatigue before you notice it in a score. Errors creep in during the second half of a timed test, your shoulders rise, or you start looking down to re-find the home row. Treat those as signals to reset your setup, not to push harder.

## A one-minute posture check

Run through this list before you start a session:

| Check | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Chair and feet | Feet flat on the floor or a footrest; thighs roughly level; back supported |
| Elbows | Close to your body and bent near a right angle, with forearms roughly parallel to the keyboard |
| Wrists | Straight and neutral, not bent upward, downward, or sideways; hovering or lightly supported, not pressed into a hard edge |
| Shoulders | Relaxed and level; not raised toward your ears or rounded forward |
| Screen | Top of the display at or a little below eye level, about an arm's length away, so you read the text without tilting your head |
| Keyboard | Centered on your body, with the home row directly in front of you |

These points follow widely used workstation guidance, including the [OSHA Computer Workstations eTool](https://www.osha.gov/etools/computer-workstations). Suitable heights and distances vary with your body and furniture, so adjust until the position feels neutral rather than matching a fixed measurement.

## Hand position on the keyboard

Start with the [home row](/blog/home-row-typing-finger-placement/): left fingers on **A S D F**, right fingers on **J K L ;**, thumbs near the space bar. The raised bumps on F and J let you find this position without looking.

Keep your fingers slightly curved, as if resting on a ball, and let them hang over the keys rather than flattening out. Reach with the finger, not the whole hand: when you type a top-row letter, the other fingers should stay close to home so you can return without searching.

Use a light touch. Pressing keys to the bottom with force does not make them register more reliably, and it tires your fingers faster. If you catch yourself hammering the keys during a race or a timed test, slow down for a few words and re-establish a softer rhythm.

## Laptop typists

A laptop keyboard and screen are attached, so a position that is right for your hands often leaves the screen too low, and a screen at eye level puts the keyboard too high. For short sessions this is usually fine. For regular practice, an external keyboard with the laptop raised, or an external display, lets you set both heights independently.

Check your wrists carefully on a laptop: resting them on a sharp front edge while typing is a common source of pressure. Our note on [keyboard covers](/blog/are-keyboard-covers-bad-for-typing/) covers another laptop-specific question.

## Breaks and session length

Short, regular sessions fit touch typing practice better than one long block. Several of NinjaType's modes end naturally: a one-minute [timed test](/), a single [finger lesson](/learn/), or a 15-word [Ghost Race](/ghost-race/) track. Use those endings as natural pauses to drop your hands, roll your shoulders, and look away from the screen.

Stop a session when accuracy falls apart or your hands feel tense. Returning rested usually produces a more useful comparison in [History](/history/) than grinding through more attempts with a declining result.

## Posture and your results

Treat posture changes like any other practice experiment, as described in the [typing speed plateau guide](/blog/typing-speed-plateau/): change one thing, keep the test settings the same, and compare several sessions rather than a single attempt. A new chair height will not show up as an instant WPM jump, but you may notice steadier accuracy late in a test or more comfortable, longer practice.

For the technique itself, continue with [touch typing for beginners](/blog/touch-typing-for-beginners/) and the [accuracy guide](/blog/improve-typing-accuracy/). Once your setup feels neutral, the remaining work is familiar: small key groups, light consistent movements, and fresh words.
