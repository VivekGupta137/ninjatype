## 1. Which free typing website should I use?

I would choose based on what you need to practise rather than pick one universal “best” site.

If you are still searching for keys, look for a site with finger-position exercises and gradual lessons. If you already know the keyboard but make mistakes when you speed up, choose a repeatable typing test that reports accuracy alongside WPM. If motivation is the problem, a typing race can make practice more engaging.

TypeRacer is one option to explore for races and text collections. Typing.com is worth exploring for structured lessons. Check their current features before choosing, because a racing game and a learning course solve different problems.

For transparency, I’m building [NinjaType](https://www.ninjatype.com/). It combines timed practice, finger lessons, and race modes. Its practice timers are 25 seconds, 45 seconds, one minute, two minutes, and five minutes; completed history stays in your browser.

Whichever tool you use, try the same test settings for a week. Note both speed and accuracy, pick one recurring error, and practise that movement slowly before retesting. That makes improvement easier to assess than chasing a different short-test score every day.

## 2. Are there alternatives to TypeRacer for racing with friends?

First decide whether you want everyone to finish the same passage, or everyone to type for the same length of time. Those are different race formats.

A passage race gives you a clear finish line. A timed race can suit a group with mixed typing speeds: everyone keeps practising until the shared timer ends, and you compare the correct text typed during that window.

TypeRacer itself offers a friend-racetrack option, so it is worth checking whether that already fits your group. If you want another room-based option, I’m building [NinjaType’s typing race](https://www.ninjatype.com/multiplayer-typing-race/).

On NinjaType, choose Race with friends, create or join a named room, complete the security check, and choose settings in the active lobby. Friends can join using the invitation or the exact room name. Words mode offers 15-, 30-, or 50-word races. Time mode offers 25 seconds through five minutes. Results include final scores and a performance chart, with a rematch option.

My practical recommendation is to agree on vocabulary and duration first. A common-word sprint and a punctuation-heavy passage can produce different results, so compare scores within the same format rather than across unrelated games.

## 3. Can Nitro Type teach me touch typing?

A typing race can help you practise keyboard movements, but finding keys confidently is a separate skill from racing through a passage quickly.

Nitro Type’s official site presents it as competitive typing practice and links to Typing.com’s lessons. That distinction is useful: a game can motivate repetition, while a lesson can help you learn where a key belongs. If you are still looking down for most letters, I would start with deliberate finger exercises before expecting a race to fix the habit.

Try a short sequence: practise a small group of keys without rushing, add common words using those keys, then play a few races to use the movements at a faster pace. If accuracy drops sharply in a race, slow down and identify the repeated mistake rather than simply starting another rushed attempt.

I’m building NinjaType, which includes [finger-specific touch typing lessons](https://www.ninjatype.com/learn/). They separate index, middle, ring, and pinky practice so you can focus on one part of the keyboard. It is another option to try alongside whichever racing game keeps you motivated.

Judge progress by similar exercises over time. A more comfortable, accurate movement is valuable even before it produces a higher WPM score.

## 4. Do typing games actually improve typing speed?

Typing games can make you practise more often, which is useful. They are most helpful when you use them deliberately rather than treat every round as a reason to rush.

For example, if you regularly miss a letter or insert an extra space, a fast race may repeatedly expose the problem. Improvement comes from noticing it, practising the movement more slowly, and then trying it again under race conditions. The game provides feedback and motivation; it does not automatically correct your technique.

I would combine three things: a consistent baseline test, a short exercise for your recurring mistakes, and a few races for variety. Keep accuracy visible. A speed increase accompanied by many more errors may not transfer well to ordinary writing.

I’m building NinjaType, and one feature is [Ghost Race](https://www.ninjatype.com/ghost-race/), which lets you chase the average WPM of your fastest valid saved attempt. The shadow’s progress is underlined in the passage. It is a personal pacing challenge, not a replay of every past keystroke.

Whatever game you choose, compare similar durations and text difficulty. There is no guaranteed number of WPM gained from a particular game or a fixed number of races.

## 5. What is a good way to organize a typing competition with friends?

Keep the first competition simple: use the same vocabulary, the same race format, and enough time for everyone to understand the rules.

For a group with different speeds, I would start with a one-minute timed round. Everyone practices for the same length of time. Afterward, compare accuracy as well as the amount of correct text typed. A short word sprint works well when the group enjoys a clear finish line, but it can end quickly if one person is much faster.

Agree on whether punctuation, numbers, and unfamiliar words will be included. Do not compare a common-word round directly with a difficult quotation and assume the WPM difference measures improvement.

You can choose Words or Time mode and a dictionary in the lobby, share an invitation, ready up, and review the final scores and performance chart. Rematches keep the room’s settings.

For a friendly training session, finish by having each person name one recurring error to practise next. That gives the competition a useful outcome beyond identifying the fastest typist.
